## Problem

A scrubbing pass had to run over a multi-terabyte corpus of environment interaction data before
that data could be handed to downstream consumers. The pass was embarrassingly parallel — each
worker takes a slice of the corpus, scrubs it, writes the result — so the obvious lever was more
workers.

That lever stopped working. Past roughly 800 workers, adding more did not reduce wall-clock time,
and the end-to-end run sat at about 24 hours. A job that has no logical dependency between its units
of work should not have a concurrency ceiling, so the ceiling was coming from somewhere other than
the work itself.

## Constraints

- The scrub itself could not change. Output had to stay byte-identical to what downstream consumers
  already validated against.
- The corpus was read-only and shared; workers could not coordinate through it.
- Scale-up had to be reversible — a bad run at 8,000 workers should not be more expensive to
  recover from than a bad run at 800.

## Why more workers stopped helping

Every worker first had to fetch the manifest describing its slice of the corpus. All of them fetched
it from the same place. That single source has a fixed egress budget, and a fixed budget divided
across a growing number of consumers gives each consumer a smaller share.

So the job had two phases with opposite scaling behaviour: the scrub scaled linearly with workers,
and the manifest fetch scaled *inversely*. Past a certain pool size, new workers spent longer
waiting on the manifest than the pool saved by having them. Wall-clock time flattened, then got
slightly worse. The bottleneck was distribution, not computation.

## Approach

Rather than scale the source, remove the idea of a single source. Distribution runs in tiers: a
small first tier pulls the manifest directly, and once a node has it, that node becomes a source for
the next tier. Each tier multiplies the available serving capacity, so capacity grows alongside the
worker pool instead of staying pinned to one host.

<FanOutTree />

The fan-out is what makes the depth manageable: one seed serves ten, those ten serve a hundred,
those hundred serve a thousand. Reaching 8,000 workers takes four tiers, not 8,000 requests against
one host.

## Tradeoffs

Tree distribution buys throughput and pays for it in latency and failure surface. The last tier
cannot start until its parent has finished receiving, so the *first* worker starts later than it
would in the flat design — the win is entirely in the tail, and only shows up at scale. A flat
design would still be the right answer for a small pool.

It also introduces dependency between workers where there was none. In the flat design a failed
fetch affected one worker. In the tree, a node that dies mid-transfer can strand everything beneath
it, which turns a trivially retryable operation into one that needs a reparenting or fallback path.

## What I would do differently

I would measure the distribution phase separately from the start. The reason the ceiling was
puzzling for as long as it was is that the two phases were timed together, and a job that is 95%
compute by design does not look like a distribution problem until you split the timer.
