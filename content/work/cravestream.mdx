## Problem

A short-form vertical feed is mostly a media-delivery problem wearing a product's clothes. The
interaction pattern is unforgiving: users scroll continuously and expect the next video to be playing
immediately, so anything that adds latency between scroll and playback is felt directly.

Layered on that, the platform had two classes of user — viewers and content partners — with genuinely
different permissions, which makes authorisation a first-class concern rather than a login screen.

## Constraints

- Playback had to start fast enough that scrolling never stalls.
- Front end and API were deployed to different providers (Vercel and Render), so every authenticated
  request was cross-origin.
- Partner-only capabilities had to be enforced server-side.

## Approach

**Media.** Rather than serve uploads directly, media goes through ImageKit, so transformation and
delivery are handled at the edge instead of by the application. The application stores references; the
CDN does the work that determines whether the feed feels fast.

**Auth.** Tokens live in `HttpOnly` cookies rather than `localStorage`. The reasoning is specific: a
token readable by JavaScript is exfiltratable by any successful XSS, whereas an `HttpOnly` cookie is
not readable by page scripts at all. This narrows XSS impact but shifts the burden to CSRF defence,
because cookies are attached automatically — a trade, not a free win.

**Split-origin deployment.** Front end and API on different hosts means cookies must be explicitly
scoped and CORS configured to permit credentialed requests from exactly one origin. This is the part
that consumed disproportionate time, and it is worth being honest that the difficulty was
configuration rather than design.

**Authorisation.** Roles are checked server-side per request, so partner capabilities do not depend on
the client hiding a button.

## Tradeoffs

Pushing media to a third-party CDN buys performance and costs control and a vendor dependency. Cookie
auth trades XSS exposure for CSRF surface. Splitting deployment across two providers buys independent
scaling and free tiers, and costs a permanent cross-origin configuration burden that a single origin
would not have.
